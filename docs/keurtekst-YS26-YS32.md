**Titel:** Why is junk email called "spam"? Blame a comedy sketch  
**Lengte:** 45 s · map `2026-12-02-YS26-spam`

**Gesproken tekst:**

> Junk email is named after a comedy sketch. 1970. A Monty Python sketch, set in a restaurant with a very limited menu. And a chorus of Vikings, chanting the word Spam, over and over. So spam came to mean something that keeps repeating, to great annoyance. Late nineteen-eighties. Players of online text games used it for flooding a chat with text. April 12, 1994. Two lawyers post one ad to thousands of Usenet newsgroups at once. Thousands of users hit back: by email, fax and phone. That ad is what first made the word spam widely known. The maker of the canned meat first defended its brand. Then it chose a sense of humor. A tin of meat, now in every inbox.

## YS27 · donderdag 3 december 2026

**Titel:** Why does an hour have 60 minutes? Not 10, not 100  
**Lengte:** 48 s · map `2026-12-03-YS27-zestig-minuten`

**Gesproken tekst:**

> Why does an hour have sixty minutes? Not ten. Not a hundred. The number comes from ancient Mesopotamia. The Babylonians counted in base sixty, from the Sumerians. Why sixty? Nobody knows for sure. But sixty splits nicely: by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30. The hours came from Egypt. Twelve parts for the light, twelve for the dark. A twenty-four hour day. Around the year 150, the astronomer Ptolemy split each degree into sixty parts. And each of those into sixty again. The first small part, in Latin: partes minutae primae. The minute. The second small part: partes minutae secundae. The second. But clocks didn't count minutes and seconds until many centuries later. Your clock still counts in sixties.

**Bronnen:**
- Michael A. Lombardi (NIST), "Why is a minute divided into 60 seconds, an hour into 60 minutes, yet there are only 24 hours in a day?", Scientific American (March 5, 2007) — https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/experts-time-division-days-hours-minutes/
- BBC News, "The ancient reason there are 60 minutes in an hour" (March 29, 2026, via The Star) — https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/world/2026-03-29-the-ancient-reason-there-are-60-minutes-in-an-hour

**Bewust niet gezegd / voorzichtig:** Niet gezegd: dat de Babyloniërs het uur in 60 minuten deelden (de bronnen zeggen dat de minuut en seconde uit de Griekse astronomie komen en pas veel later voor de klok gebruikt werden), en de populaire uitleg over tellen op vingerkootjes (niet in deze bronnen). **Titel:** Why is a marathon exactly 42.195 km (26.2 miles)?  
**Lengte:** 42 s · map `2026-12-04-YS28-marathon`

**Gesproken tekst:**

> A marathon is forty-two point one nine five kilometres. Why that odd number? London, 1908. The Olympic marathon. The start: by the East Terrace of Windsor Castle. The finish: in front of the Royal Box, in the White City Stadium. That course was precisely twenty-six miles, three hundred and eighty-five yards. Or forty-two point one nine five kilometres. A popular story says the royals wanted that start. One Olympic history site calls that certainly false. In 1921, the I-A-A-F made it the official marathon distance. Marathons today still run that 1908 London distance. A castle and a stadium set the distance.

## YS29 · zaterdag 5 december 2026

**Titel:** Why does a phone keypad start with 1-2-3, but a calculator with 7-8-9?  
**Lengte:** 43 s · map `2026-12-05-YS29-toetsenblok`

**Gesproken tekst:**

> Your phone puts 1, 2, 3 on top. Your calculator puts 7, 8, 9 on top. Why? 1960. Bell engineers prepare a new push-button telephone. They tested sixteen different arrangements of the keys. One was the layout found on adding machines, with seven, eight, nine on top. It was not the best of its group. The same shape, numbered with 1, 2, 3 on top, was faster to key. But only a little. 4.92 seconds, against 5.08. So the push-button phone got 1, 2, 3 on top. Not at the bottom, as on a calculator. And why adding machines had seven on top? The study doesn't say. Same ten digits. Two different answers.

**Bronnen:**
- R. L. Deininger, "Human Factors Engineering Studies of the Design and Use of Pushbutton Telephone Sets", Bell System Technical Journal 39(4), July 1960 (scan) — https://www.vcalc.net/Documents/touchtone_hf.pdf

**Titel:** The microwave oven was discovered by a melted candy bar  
**Lengte:** 49 s · map `2026-12-06-YS30-magnetron`

**Gesproken tekst:**

> It was a mistake. A candy bar melted in an engineer's pocket. Percy Spencer worked with magnetrons: tubes that produce microwave energy. He didn't invent the tube. He found a way to mass produce it. Standing near one, he noticed the candy bar in his pocket had started to melt. Chocolate, says one story. A peanut bar, says his grandson. He tried popcorn kernels. They popped all over his lab. Then an egg. It exploded. October 8, 1945: a patent for cooking food with microwaves. The first microwave oven, in 1947, stood about six feet tall, and weighed about 750 pounds. In 1967, a countertop model for the home sold for 495 dollars. A melted candy bar started it.

**Titel:** Why do we change the clocks? It started in World War I  
**Lengte:** 46 s · map `2026-12-07-YS31-zomertijd`

**Gesproken tekst:**

> Twice a year we move the clocks. It started with a war. Not with Benjamin Franklin. In 1784 he wrote a joke: fire cannons in the streets to wake people up. He never invented moving the clocks. 1895, New Zealand. Insect collector George Hudson wants more daylight after work. Move the clocks two hours each spring. The local science society ridiculed his idea. 1907, London. William Willett pays for a pamphlet himself: The Waste of Daylight. Then the war. April 30, 1916: Germany and Austria-Hungary move the clocks first. To save coal. Britain follows: its law passes on May 17, 1916. You still change your clock for a wartime idea.

## YS32 · dinsdag 8 december 2026

**Titel:** Monopoly began as a game against landlords  
**Lengte:** 36 s · map `2026-12-08-YS32-monopoly`

**Gesproken tekst:**

> Monopoly began as a warning about landlords. 1904. Lizzie Magie patents a game: The Landlord's Game. She followed Henry George, who wanted a single tax on land. Her game was meant to show the tragic effects of land-grabbing. An argument against the concentration of wealth. Then the Great Depression. Charles Darrow, out of work, made copies of Monopoly at home. He got the game through a friend, and sold it to Parker Brothers as his own. 1935. Parker Brothers buys the rights, and sales skyrocket. A game against monopolies, sold as Monopoly.
